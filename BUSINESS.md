# SAKHI Business Model

**The core rule:** everything a woman needs to stay safe is free, always. Institutions that benefit when she is healthier pay to deploy SAKHI. Optional extras and a sponsored **Care Store** for low-cost pads and essentials bring in more revenue.

All prices below are early estimates to test with partners, not final.

---

## 1. Free forever

These features are never charged for. Putting safety behind a paywall would cost the trust of government partners, NGOs and users.

- Period care, cycle tracking, the change timer and myth cards
- Red-flag alerts, emergency numbers and family alert
- Pregnancy danger signs, government scheme matching and vaccine dates
- The offline chat
- Downloading or deleting her own data
- The free option in the Care Store, shown first: Jan Aushadhi and free government supply

## 2. Revenue streams

### 2.1 Government (B2G): main long-term customer

| | |
|---|---|
| **What we sell** | SAKHI deployed across a district or state, plus an **ASHA-worker dashboard**. With consent, it shows red flags, missed antenatal visits, women due for an Hb test, and supplies needed in her area. |
| **Why they pay** | It directly supports goals they are already funded and measured on: Anaemia Mukt Bharat, menstrual hygiene, antenatal care, immunisation. |
| **Pricing** | Setup about ₹5–15 lakh per district (training and content in local languages), then about ₹10–30 per woman per year, or a flat district licence. |
| **Reality check** | Government contracts take 6–18 months and go through tenders. Start with a funded pilot. |

### 2.2 CSR sponsorship: fastest route to the first money

| | |
|---|---|
| **What we sell** | "Sponsor SAKHI in a district." The company funds the roll-out and receives impact reports: women reached, pads distributed, anaemia referrals. |
| **Why they pay** | Larger companies must spend 2% of profits on CSR. Women's health, hygiene and sanitation are eligible categories. |
| **Pricing** | About ₹20–50 lakh a year per district programme. |
| **Likely sponsors** | Pad and hygiene brands, pharma, banks, FMCG companies. |

### 2.3 Private clinics and hospitals: patient-tracking dashboard

The **main private revenue stream**: a dashboard that lets a clinic follow its own patients between visits. It mirrors the ASHA dashboard sold to government, but is aimed at private gynaecologists.

**Why they would pay:** a hospital doesn't pay for "an app"; it pays for results.

| Clinic's problem | What SAKHI gives them |
|---|---|
| Pregnant patients miss antenatal visits and deliver elsewhere | Visit reminders and follow-up, so patients stay with the clinic (one delivery is worth about ₹30,000–₹1.5 lakh to a private hospital) |
| High-risk cases are spotted late | A daily list of patients with high BP, low Hb or reduced baby movement, between visits |
| Doctors have little history at the first visit | The doctor summary: cycles, readings, labs, tablets, mood |
| Patients forget iron and calcium tablets | Tablet-ledger adherence for each patient |
| Women with PCOS or heavy periods don't return | Gentle reminders to come back |

**Pitch:** "Fewer missed visits, fewer emergencies arriving late, more deliveries at your hospital."

**Who to sell to:**

| Customer | Fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Private gynaecology clinics and nursing homes (10–50 beds)** | **Start here** | One doctor decides, they compete for deliveries, and they can start in weeks |
| Mid-size maternity chains | Later | Bigger deals, but slow decisions; they will want it linked to their hospital software |
| Government hospitals and PHCs | Through NHM (section 2.1), not direct sales | Most of their data already goes into the RCH portal and ANMOL |
| NGO hospitals | White-label licence (below) | They want their own branding and helpline |

**Pricing to test:**

| Plan | Price |
|---|---|
| Clinic plan | ₹1,500–₹3,000 a month for up to 200 active patients |
| Per pregnancy | ₹50–₹150 per pregnant patient, paid once for the whole pregnancy |
| Hospital plan | ₹25,000–₹1 lakh a year, plus setup |
| White-label (NGOs and hospitals) | About ₹2–10 lakh a year: their logo, helpline and doctors in the referral list, plus a per-user fee above an agreed number of users |

The woman never pays.

**Rough check:** 50 clinics × ₹2,000 a month is about ₹12 lakh a year, enough to run SAKHI. 200 clinics is about ₹48 lakh a year.

**How it works in the app:**
1. She scans the clinic's QR code and taps "Share my readings with Dr X's clinic". Sharing happens only with her consent, and she can stop it at any time.
2. The clinic sees a **one-minute daily list**: who has red flags, who missed a visit, who isn't taking tablets.
3. She gets clinic-branded reminders, such as "Your next visit at Sri Clinic is on 4 Dec."
4. Most of this is already built on her side: risk tiles, MCP and ASHA readings, the tablet ledger and the doctor summary. **What's new is the clinic dashboard and the consent-to-share step.**

**Rules and risks:**

| Risk | How we handle it |
|---|---|
| Data protection (DPDP Act 2023) | Explicit consent per clinic. She can stop sharing at any time. Only the readings needed are shared. Access is logged. |
| Medical device rules (CDSCO) | Present it as monitoring and reminders, with the doctor deciding. Get regulatory advice before charging. |
| Her data stays hers | If she changes doctor, her record goes with her, not with the clinic. No clinic can see women who haven't joined it. |
| Busy doctors | The dashboard must take one minute a day. |
| Linking with hospital software | Plan ABHA (Ayushman Bharat health record) support later. It also helps with government sales. |

**First test:** ask the reviewing gynaecologist: "Would your clinic use this dashboard, and would you pay ₹2,000 a month for it?" If yes, ask whether 3–5 of their patients could try it.

### 2.4 Optional extras for individual users

| Feature | Price idea |
|---|---|
| Teleconsultation with a gynaecologist, booked from the doctor summary | ₹199–399 per consult; SAKHI keeps 15–20% |
| Detailed reports (PCOS pattern report, trimester report as a PDF) | ₹49–99 each |
| Family plan: alerts and a shared pregnancy view for her husband or mother | ₹49 per month |
| Unlimited AI chat beyond the free daily limit | ₹29–49 per month |

Expect a small audience at first, mostly women in cities. Treat this as extra income, not the base.

### 2.5 Referrals

- **Lab tests:** when the iron check suggests an Hb test, show partner labs that collect samples at home. SAKHI earns about 10–15% referral commission.
- **Rule:** the free government option (PHC, Anganwadi, Jan Aushadhi) is always shown first, and paid partners are clearly labelled.

### 2.6 Anonymised insights: later, only with consent

Combined, non-identifiable trends, such as anaemia signs by district, could be offered to health departments and research institutions. This needs explicit consent, DPDP Act compliance and an ethics review. It is not part of the current pitch.

## 3. SAKHI Care Store: low-cost pads and essentials

**First partner (November 2026):** Aram Cotton Napkins, a women-led cotton pad maker in Pallipalayam, Erode (4.9 stars from 293 Google reviews; packs of 16 to 24 pads for about ₹75). They agreed to be listed in SAKHI. They are shown in Period care after the free and ₹1 options, with no health claims. Commission and terms are not agreed yet.

### The idea

The Period care card already tells her when supplies are running low. The Care Store lets her act on that inside the app. She can order or reserve low-cost pads and other essentials, sold by:

- **Government channels**, if the state chooses: Jan Aushadhi Suvidha pads, or state menstrual-hygiene scheme supply through ASHA workers, schools and Anganwadis.
- **Sponsors:** pad brands, cup makers, women's self-help groups making cloth pads, and pharma companies (IFA tablets, calcium, folic acid where sold over the counter).
- **CSR-funded free stock:** a sponsor pays, she pays nothing.

### What it would sell

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Period products | Disposable pads, biodegradable pads, reusable cloth pads (often made by self-help groups), menstrual cups, period underwear |
| Hygiene | Intimate wash (gentle, not perfumed), disposal bags, a cup sterilising container |
| Nutrition | Iron-rich foods, such as ragi mixes and jaggery-sesame bars from self-help groups |
| Pregnancy and baby | Hospital bag items, maternity pads, nursing pads, baby care basics |
| Over-the-counter supplements | Only if allowed, and only alongside the tablet ledger. Never prescription medicines. |

### How she gets it (the three delivery models)

| Model | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Pick up from her ASHA worker** | She reserves in the app; her ASHA worker or the Anganwadi hands it over. The worker gets a small incentive per handover. | Villages, free or government stock |
| **Collect at a partner store** | Jan Aushadhi Kendra, a pharmacy or a self-help-group shop. She shows a code in the app. | Small towns, cheap paid stock |
| **Home delivery** | A sponsor or e-commerce partner delivers to her door. | Cities, paid brands |

### How it fits into the app

1. The supply check sees she has 6 pads and her period is in 3 days. It shows **"Get more"**.
2. The store opens with **the free or cheapest option first** (government or CSR stock), then sponsored options, clearly labelled **"Sponsored"**.
3. She picks delivery: ASHA pickup, store pickup or home.
4. Cash on delivery or UPI. Free stock needs no payment.
5. Her pad count updates automatically when she confirms she received the order.
6. Discreet mode applies: neutral item names in notifications, and discreet packaging from partners.

### How SAKHI earns from the store

| Source | Amount |
|---|---|
| Commission on sponsor sales | About 5–15% per order |
| Sponsor listing or placement fee | A fixed monthly fee for a brand to appear (always labelled "Sponsored") |
| Government or CSR distribution fee | A per-unit or per-district fee for running the ordering and tracking for free stock |
| Data reports for distributors | Combined, anonymous demand by area (how many pads are needed where), to help stock planning |

### Rules that protect trust

- **Free and cheapest first, always.** Sponsors never outrank free government supply.
- **Clear labels.** Sponsored items say so.
- **No pressure.** The store never appears in emergencies, mood checks or red-flag screens.
- **Health claims checked.** Products must meet BIS standards where they apply (for example sanitary pads). No product makes medical claims we haven't checked.
- **Privacy.** Order history isn't shared with sponsors. Sponsors only see combined, anonymous numbers.
- **Self-help groups get a fair place.** Reusable cloth pads made by local women's groups get free listings.

### What we need to build it

1. Product catalogue and sponsor portal: partners add products, prices and stock by pin code.
2. Store screen linked from the supply check.
3. Orders with pickup codes and ASHA-worker handover.
4. UPI and cash on delivery through a payment gateway, plus a free-stock path that needs no payment.
5. An admin dashboard for government or CSR partners showing distribution reports.
6. Legal: e-commerce rules, GST, the legal entity that sells, and returns and refunds.

**Phase 1 suggestion:** start with **free CSR-sponsored pads picked up from ASHA workers** in the pilot district. No payments, no delivery logistics, and it proves demand. Add paid options and delivery later.

## 4. Costs

These estimates still need checking against current prices.

**Assumptions:**
- About 30 AI replies per user per month.
- Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite.
- Vercel hosting and managed PostgreSQL.
- ₹85 to the dollar.

| Users | Running cost per user per month |
|---|---|
| 1,000 | about ₹5 |
| 10,000 | about ₹1.5 |
| 1,00,000 | about ₹1.4 |

Training, support, content translation and the team are extra. To cover everything, a B2G or CSR deal needs about **₹50–60 per woman per year**.

**How costs stay low:**
- The offline engine handles many messages without AI.
- A per-user daily limit caps AI spending.
- An optional local model removes AI costs entirely.

## 5. Order of execution

| When | Step |
|---|---|
| Now | One CSR- or NGO-funded pilot in one district, with free sponsored pads picked up from ASHA workers through the Care Store |
| 3–6 months | Clinic dashboard prototype; 3–5 private gynaecology clinics try it, starting with the reviewing gynaecologist |
| 6–12 months | Take the pilot results to state NHM for a B2G contract. Start charging clinics (target 50). Add teleconsultation, lab referrals and paid Care Store options. |
| 12–24 months | White-label licences, the family plan, home delivery, more states |
| Later | Anonymised insights, only with consent and ethics approval |

## 6. Pitch line

> "SAKHI is free for every woman. Governments, CSR partners and NGOs pay to deploy it, because it helps them meet the anaemia, menstrual hygiene and maternal health goals they are already funded for. Private clinics pay for a dashboard that keeps their pregnant patients safe and coming back between visits. The SAKHI Care Store turns a 'you're running low' reminder into free or low-cost pads from her ASHA worker or a sponsor, with free options always first."

## 7. Open questions for the team

- Which legal entity will run sales and take payments: a company, a Section 8 non-profit, or a partner that sells on our behalf?
- Which CSR sponsor and which district for the pilot?
- Does the target state already run a free-pad scheme that the Care Store can plug into?
- How should ASHA workers be paid for handovers: through the sponsor, through NHM, or as a fee per handover?
- Will clinics pay ₹1,500–₹3,000 a month? Ask the reviewing gynaecologist and 3–5 other clinics before fixing the price.
- Do we need regulatory advice (CDSCO) before charging clinics for risk alerts?
